Burke and Wills, 1861 April–September 1861

Read the notebook page through to the last line.

Friday, 28 June 1861.

Cooper Creek

Clear, cold night; slight breeze from the E.; day beautifully warm and pleasant. Mr Burke suffers greatly from the cold, and is getting extremely weak. He and King start tomorrow up the creek … it is the only chance we have of being saved from starvation.

I am weaker than ever, although I have a good appetite and relish the nardoo much; but it seems to give us no nutriment …

Nothing now but the greatest good luck can save any of us; and as for myself I may live four or five days if the weather continues warm. My pulse is at forty-eight, and very weak, and my legs and arms are nearly skin and bone. I can only look out, like Mr Micawber, “for something to turn up.” Starvation on nardoo is by no means very unpleasant, but for the weakness one feels, and the utter inability to move oneself; for as far as appetite is concerned, it gives me the greatest satisfaction.

W. J. Wills

CASE FILE: BURKE AND WILLS / 1861

Date of incident
April–September 1861 (depot: 21 April; last notebook: late June; King found: 15 September)
Location
Cooper Creek, in Yandruwandha country
Status
Unresolved contradictions

Key claims still disputed

  • What was the real cause of death — hunger, the missed depot, the nardoo itself, the cold, or more than one of these?
  • Whose record should we trust — Wills’s notebook, King’s later narrative, or Howitt’s diary of the rescue?
  • What does the 1861 file leave unasked about the Yandruwandha people who kept King alive?

What is agreed · from the 1861 file, and after

  • In 1860 a party left Melbourne to cross the continent from south to north. Robert O’Hara Burke led it. William John Wills was the surveyor. John King and Charles Gray went north with them from the Cooper Creek depot. (Some newspapers of the time wrote Grey. The expedition records usually write Gray.)
  • The four reached the Gulf country and turned back. Gray died on 17 April 1861, on the return, before they reached the depot.
  • On the evening of 21 April 1861, Burke, Wills and King reached the depot with two camels and almost no food. The depot party, led by William Brahe, had left that same morning. Stores had been buried, and a tree was marked so a returning party would know to dig. The exact wording cut into the tree is uncertain: sources disagree.
  • They tried to make south towards Mount Hopeless, then came back to the creek. Both remaining camels died. They tried to live on nardoo — a fern seed used as food by the Yandruwandha people of the Cooper — and on fish and gifts they were given.
  • On 30 May 1861 Wills went back to the depot and left a note in the buried cache. The depot was still empty. Unseen by him, Brahe and Wright had visited the cache earlier in May, thought it untouched, and left again. The wording of that 30 May note is in Super Sleuths. It is not the same document as the last notebook page at the front of this case.
  • Wills’s last notebook entry is from late June 1861. He wrote that nardoo seemed to give them “no nutriment,” that his pulse was at forty-eight, and that he could only look out, like Mr Micawber, “for something to turn up.” Burke and King then went up the creek for help, leaving food and water with him.
  • Burke died a short time later. King later found Wills already dead. The exact days are not known. King was found alive on 15 September 1861 by Alfred Howitt’s relief party, after living with Yandruwandha people along the creek.
  • In 1994, J. W. Earl and B. V. McCleary argued in the journal Nature that nardoo can destroy the vitamin thiamine, and that Burke and Wills may have died of beriberi as well as of hunger and cold. That is a later scientific reading, not something the 1861 file could know. It is still debated.

Green on this page marks what can be agreed. Amber and other colours, used in Super Sleuths, mark what is still disputed or was added later.

William John Wills

Surveyor of the expedition · 1834–1861

His Cooper Creek field notebook, recovered after his death.

Source. National Library of Australia, MS 30/7, journal of the trip from Cooper Creek towards Adelaide, 23 April to late June 1861. A widely printed wording of the last entry is used on the opening page and on this card; date and wording variants are set out in the teacher notes.

What they said. In the last entry he wrote that he still had an appetite and relished the nardoo, “but it seems to give us no nutriment.” He thought he might live four or five days if the weather stayed warm. “My pulse is at forty-eight, and very weak, and my legs and arms are nearly skin and bone. I can only look out, like Mr Micawber, ‘for something to turn up.’” A few days earlier he had written that he could not understand the nardoo at all: it would not agree with him in any form.

Could they really have seen it? He was there, writing in his own notebook as his strength failed. He could record what he felt and what he ate. He could not see what would happen after Burke and King left, and he could not test, as later scientists have tried to, why the nardoo seemed to give no nutriment.

Why might they say it? To leave a record, and a last message, in case someone came. A man writing as he grows weaker is close to the event, but he is not a doctor, and he is writing his own ending.

Your notes

John King

The only member of the northern party to survive · 1838–1872

A narrative taken down for Howitt at Cooper Creek in September 1861, later given to the Commission of Enquiry.

Source. “Narrative of John King,” written up by Alfred Howitt from notes in his field-book, State Library of Victoria, MS 13071. King told it weeks after the deaths, when he was still very weak.

What they said. He said the three of them reached the depot on the evening of 21 April and found that the party had gone the same day. They dug up the buried stores. Later, when they were too weak to keep collecting food, Wills asked to be left with a supply, and they buried the rest of his field-books by his hut. Burke asked him again whether he still wished it. King said that Burke, on the last evening, asked him to stay until he was dead, and to leave him as he lay. King found him gone the next morning. Days later he found Wills already dead, and later still he lived on what Yandruwandha people gave him until the relief party came.

Could they really have seen it? He was the only one of the three still alive. He had been at the depot on 21 April, on the last walk with Burke, and at Wills’s hut when he returned. No one else from the party could check his account. He told it after weeks of hunger, and Howitt wrote it down.

Why might they say it? Howitt needed a report to take back to Melbourne, and King was the only survivor who had been there. Memory, weakness, and the wish to explain what had happened can all shape a story told after the event.

Your notes

Alfred Howitt

Leader of the Victorian relief party · 1830–1908

His own diary of the day his party found King.

Source. A. W. Howitt, diary of the Victorian Contingent Expedition, State Library of Victoria, MS 13071, entry for Sunday, 15 September 1861. Published in the Melbourne press after the party returned.

What they said. On 15 September 1861 Howitt wrote that he was still following tracks when two of his party came with the news that King, the only survivor of Burke’s party, had been found. He went across to the huts and found King sitting in a hut that the people of the creek had made for him. King, he wrote, “presented a melancholy appearance — wasted to a shadow,” and seemed exceedingly weak, so that Howitt sometimes found it hard to follow what he said. The people of the camp were gathered round, looking, Howitt wrote, gratified and delighted. He decided to stay about ten days to let King recover before starting back.

Could they really have seen it? He was there the day King was found, and he wrote it in his diary that same day. He did not see Burke or Wills die, and he was not the first of his party to reach King: others found him, and Howitt came across a little later. What he records of the deaths is what King told him.

Why might they say it? He had been sent to find the missing party and to report. A diary written the same day is close to the rescue. It is still one man’s view, and it is not a Yandruwandha account of the same day.

Why it still matters

Burke and Wills are still told as a heroic crossing, or as a bungled meeting at a tree. The file is open because the records do not settle what killed them, and because the people who kept King alive have not always been asked. Content note. The language of the 1861 records

The extracts below keep a few words from 1861 records that this case file does not use as its own, including “blacks” and “natives.” They are short, attributed quotations. They are not the language of this book.

1. Two different documents

Wills wrote the 30 May 1861 cache note at the depot and buried it in the camel-box cache (State Library of Victoria, MS 13071, SAFE 1, “Last notes”). A careful transcription of the two pages reads, in part:

Depot Camp, May 30th. We have been unable to leave the creek. Both camels are dead, and our provisions are exhausted. Mr Burke and King are down the lower part of the creek. I am about to return to them, when we shall probably come up this way. We are trying to live the best way we can, like the blacks, but find it hard work. Our clothes are going to pieces fast. Send provisions and clothes as soon as possible. W J Wills. The Depot party having left, contrary to instructions, has put us in this fix. I have deposited some of my journals here for fear of accidents. W J W.

That note does not contain “my pulse is at forty-eight,” “skin and bone,” or “it seems to give us no nutriment.” Those lines are from the later field notebook (NLA MS 30/7), used at the front of this case. Why does it matter if a later writer mixes the two documents into one page?

2. The tree, and why no one was waiting

King, in his 1861 narrative, remembered a tree marked “DIG, Apr 21.” Popular tellings often give “DIG 3 FT NW.” The Dig Tree site and later writers also record date blazes (including December 1860 and 21 April 1861), a camp mark often read as B LXV (Burke, Camp 65), and a later mark by Howitt, AH DIG. A National Trust plaque notes that the Burke party recalled a depth of three feet, while some early settlers remembered forty feet. John White and later research list still more published variants. Brahe told the Commission he marked a tree with the date 21 April, and also a December date.

Why was no one waiting at the Dig Tree? That was this case’s first outlined question. Brahe had already waited about four months. One of his party, Patton, was badly hurt. On 21 April he left for the Darling — the same morning the three men came in. In early May, Brahe and Wright returned, thought the cache untouched, and left again, before Wills came back on 30 May. What can we actually agree about the tree, and what is still in dispute?

3. The 1994 Nature paper

J. W. Earl and B. V. McCleary, “Mystery of the poisoned expedition,” Nature 368 (21 April 1994): 683–684, argued that nardoo (Marsilea drummondii) contains thiaminase, an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1 (thiamine), and that Burke and Wills may have died of beriberi as well as of hunger. An earlier paper, McCleary and Chick, Phytochemistry 16 (1977), had identified thiaminase in nardoo. Yandruwandha preparation of the seed is part of the argument: the explorers may not have prepared it in the same way. John Hayman wrote a follow-up letter in Nature 370 (1994). The 1994 paper is a hypothesis, not an autopsy, and it is still debated.

Wills himself had already written that the nardoo seemed to give “no nutriment,” and that he could not understand it. How far can a later scientific paper settle a question the 1861 file left open?

4. What the 1861 file did not ask

This file does not put “the Yandruwandha people” on a witness card. A group is not one witness, Teachers should follow the protocols of Yandruwandha Yawarrawarrka and related communities, and the AIATSIS Code of Ethics, before adding a Yandruwandha witness card.

Published starting points, for reading with community guidance, include: Benny Kerwin’s Yandruwandha narrative, transcribed by Gavan Breen, in Luise Hercus and Peter Sutton (eds), This Is What Happened: Historical Narratives by Aborigines (Australian Institute of Aboriginal Studies, 1986); the listing for the Burke, Wills, King and Yandruwandha National Heritage Place (DCCEEW); Wills’s diary for 3 June 1861, in which a man he names Pitchery feeds him fish and nardoo; and King’s narrative of being fed, and of the people who wept over Burke’s remains and covered them with bushes.

What is not asked if we only use the explorers’ notebooks? Whose skill kept King alive?

5. Burke’s last request, in King’s words

In the narrative Howitt took down, King said that Burke, on the last evening, spoke these words: “I hope you will remain with me here till I am quite dead — it is a comfort to know that some one is by; but, when I am dying, it is my wish that you should place the pistol in my right hand, and that you leave me unburied as I lie.” King said that the following morning, about eight o’clock, Burke died. The student card tells the request more quietly, because this file is for younger readers. Why might Burke have asked for that? What can we know, and what can we not?

Teacher notes

The opening page is not constructed, and it should not be labelled “constructed” on the student page. It is an abridged typographic presentation of Wills’s last field-notebook entry (NLA MS 30/7). Ellipses mark two cuts, including a period-language clause, so the page can hold the nutriment, pulse, and Mr Micawber lines without mixing in the 30 May cache note. The printed date, Friday 28 June 1861, is calendar-correct (that Friday); some transcripts print 26 or 27 June. Wording also varies (“pulse is” / “pulse are”).
